# Mission Cases

**What this document is.** RAPTOR plans medical-delivery flights over Quito
under Ecuador's RDAC 101. This document lists every mission shape it can
plan, the settings each was run with, the numbers each produced, and the
command that reproduces it. It is meant to be read by someone deciding
which case fits their question, and by someone checking a number in a
paper against the run that produced it.

Every figure named here is generated by a command given in the same
section. If a figure and a number disagree, the command is the arbiter.

---

## How to read this

Each case has the same five parts:

| Part | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Question** | What the case is for — the operational decision it informs |
| **Shape** | The sequence of stops, payload changes and ground actions |
| **Settings** | Every parameter that changes the answer |
| **Result** | What came out, with the spread where the spread matters |
| **Reproduce** | The command |

### What is being optimised

**Energy, unless a case says otherwise.** The optimiser takes three
objectives — `energy`, `time` and `balanced` (a 50/50 weighted sum) —
and every mission builder defaults to energy.

Separately from the objective, a mission carries an **urgency**, which
sets a regulatory time cap and a state-of-charge floor:

| urgency | time cap per leg | SOC floor |
|---|---:|---:|
| routine | 60 min | 15 % |
| urgent | 30 min | 15 % |
| emergency | 15 min | 20 % |

The two are deliberately independent: a mission can be *constrained* in
time without being *optimised* for it, which is the normal case — a
blood delivery has a deadline and still wants to arrive with charge in
hand.

**Until this round none of that reached the optimiser.** `plan_mission`
mapped the priority to a mode and then passed neither the time cap nor
the SOC floor, so an EMERGENCY mission planned exactly like a routine
resupply. The machinery existed and was unreachable through the entry
point the missions actually use. Case 2.6 is the comparison that was
impossible before.

Two conventions run through all of it.

**Energy is reported at the pack terminals, not at the propeller.** The
figure includes the I²R heat that never reaches the drivetrain, because
that is what empties the battery. On these missions it is 1.5–2.5 % of
what leaves the cells.

**State of charge runs from 1 to 0 over the *usable* energy**, not over
the pack's nameplate capacity. `SOC min` of 15 % therefore means 15 % of
the usable energy left, with the unusable remainder still below it. A
mission that ends at SOC 0 has not flattened its battery; it has spent
its reserve.

The aircraft and the envelope are both derived rather than typed in. To


**Why the pack is 22 Ah and not the 30 Ah T-Drones also lists.** Counting
the wiring, BMS and case that a real pack carries — 15 % on top of the
cells — the 30 Ah option does not fit inside the 10.0 kg empty mass the
same manufacturer publishes. The largest listed option that closes the
mass budget is 22 Ah, and that is what ships. The reasoning, and the same
calculation for the other two aircraft, is in

This matters for reading every energy number below: **the pack is 24 %
smaller than the datasheet's largest option**, so the margins here are
tighter than a naive reading of the specification would give.

## Part 1 — Corridor cases

These ask whether a *route* is flyable, before any mission structure is
put on top of it. They are cheap and they are the right first question:
a mission built on a corridor that cannot be flown legally is not a
planning problem, it is an infrastructure problem.

### Case 1.1 — Corridor atlas

**Question.** Which pairs of facilities can be connected by a straight
corridor that stays inside the legal AGL band, and which need lateral
routing, a waiver, or different endpoints?

**Shape.** All 15 pairs among six facilities. No optimisation: the
corridor profile is built directly from the terrain and the aircraft's
climb and descent angles, and the question is whether any terrain-following
height fits inside 60–122 m AGL along the whole leg.

**Settings.** Six facilities: Hospital Eugenio Espejo, Hospital Enrique
Garcés, Hospital Pablo Arturo Suárez, Hospital Docente Calderón, Centro de
Salud Lloa, Centro de Salud Píntag. Nine candidate cruise heights swept
across the band per pair.

**Result.**

| pair | km | relief | over ceiling | fraction of leg |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| P.A. Suárez → Lloa | 17.5 | 1334 m | **434 m** | 64.8 % |
| Calderón → Lloa | 23.9 | 1247 m | 335 m | 44.4 % |
| P.A. Suárez → Píntag | 32.1 | 668 m | 310 m | 25.3 % |
| Calderón → Píntag | 32.0 | 916 m | 245 m | 25.2 % |
| Espejo → Lloa | 11.3 | 728 m | 208 m | 59.3 % |
| Espejo → Píntag | 23.4 | 624 m | 130 m | 18.5 % |
| Garcés → Píntag | 22.3 | 709 m | 124 m | 17.9 % |
| Lloa → Píntag | 27.6 | 837 m | 124 m | 24.9 % |
| Garcés → Lloa | 5.3 | 441 m | 110 m | 56.4 % |
| Espejo → Calderón | 13.7 | 332 m | 82 m | 3.7 % |
| Garcés → P.A. Suárez | 18.1 | 544 m | 61 m | 9.2 % |
| Espejo → Garcés | 9.6 | 231 m | 51 m | 9.4 % |
| Garcés → Calderón | 23.2 | 361 m | 11 m | 0.7 % |
| P.A. Suárez → Calderón | 7.6 | 237 m | **0 m** | 0 % |
| Espejo → P.A. Suárez | 9.7 | 88 m | **0 m** | 0 % |

**2 of 15 pairs admit a legal straight corridor.** The other thirteen are
where lateral routing has to earn its keep.

Two things in that table are worth more attention than the headline.

The first is that **distance predicts almost nothing.** Garcés → Lloa is
the second-shortest leg on the list at 5.3 km and busts the ceiling over
56 % of its length; Garcés → Calderón is four times longer and misses by
11 m. What matters is relief *per kilometre* — how fast the ground moves
under a corridor that has to follow it — and Lloa sits on the flank of
Pichincha.

The second is that the fraction column and the excess column disagree
about which legs are worst. P.A. Suárez → Píntag is 310 m over but only
across a quarter of the leg: one ridge, and a waiver or a detour fixes
it. Espejo → Lloa is 208 m over across 59 % of the leg: there is no
single obstacle to route around, the whole corridor is wrong.

### Case 1.2 — Planning methods compared

**Question.** What does each planning method actually buy, and what does
it cost?

**Shape.** One corridor — Garcés → Píntag, 22.3 km, 709 m of relief —
planned five ways and drawn on one figure.

**Settings.**

| method | what it is |
|---|---|
| straight line, constant altitude | no routing at all; the naive baseline |
| terrain-following, DGAC off | corridor profile follows the ground, airspace ignored |
| A* grid | 400 m cells, 5 altitude levels, energy-cost edges |
| optimised, DGAC on, N=1 | differential evolution, one intermediate waypoint |
| optimised, DGAC on, N=3 | the same with three |

Optimiser effort: 10 generations, population 8, seed 42.

**Result.**

| method | over ceiling | max lateral offset | energy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| straight line, constant altitude | **+647 m** | 0 m | — |
| terrain-following, DGAC off | +161 m | 0 m | — |
| A* grid (400 m cells) | **0 m** | 1251 m | — |
| optimised, DGAC on, N=1 | +46 m | 2664 m | 147 Wh |
| optimised, DGAC on, N=3 | **0 m** | 3941 m | 189 Wh |

Three results here, and only one of them is the expected one.

**Terrain following alone recovers three quarters of the problem.** From
+647 m to +161 m without any lateral movement at all — the aircraft
simply stops holding one altitude across 709 m of relief.

**Lateral routing recovers the rest, and it is not free.** N=3 reaches
full compliance, at 3.9 km of lateral offset and 189 Wh against N=1's
147 Wh. **More waypoints bought legality and cost 29 % more energy.**
That trade is the substance of the method, and it is invisible in a
profile view — both routes look similar from the side. The plan view and
the deviation trace are what make it legible.

**A\* found a compliant route here and differential evolution at N=1 did
not.** On a grid with a lateral extent, A* can step sideways wherever it
likes; DE with one waypoint has one degree of lateral freedom to spend.
This is not a general result — the atlas study shows corridors where the
ordering reverses — but it is a real caution against reading a single
comparison as a verdict on a method.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m scripts.make_figures --only methods --maxiter 10 --popsize 8
```

**Figure.**

![All five planning methods on one corridor](../figures/current/method_comparison.png)

*Three views of the same five routes. The profile (top right) is the

### What Part 1 adds up to

Three things, and none of them is about the aircraft.

**Feasibility is a property of the terrain, not of the distance.** The
atlas has a 5.3 km leg that fails over 56 % of its length and a 23 km one
that misses by 11 m. Anyone sizing a drone-delivery network from a map
and a range figure will get the answer wrong, and will get it wrong
optimistically, because the short legs look easy.

**The binding constraint is the ceiling, not the floor.** Every failure
in the atlas is a corridor that cannot stay *under* 122 m AGL while
following ground that climbs faster than the aircraft can. Terrain
clearance — the constraint people expect to bind — is satisfied
everywhere. That is a consequence of the rule, not of the geography: a
122 m ceiling measured above ground level turns every hill into an
obstacle for an aircraft that must both clear it and stay near it.

**Lateral routing is the tool that fixes it, and its cost is distance,
not energy.** Case 1.2's N=3 route reaches full compliance at 3.9 km of
lateral offset and 29 % more energy than the N=1 route that does not.
Whether that trade is worth making is an operational decision the model
cannot make, but it can price it — which is the point of putting the map,
the profile and the deviation trace on one page.

## Part 2 — Mission cases

These put structure on top of a corridor: several legs, payload changing
between them, and state of charge carried across.

The thing that makes a mission more than a sum of legs is that **the
second leg starts on whatever the first one left in the pack.** A round
trip is not two independent flights, and the range that matters is not
the one-way range.

### Case 2.1 — One-way delivery

**Question.** The simplest thing an operator does: get a payload from A
to B. What does it cost, and how much of the pack does it use?

**Shape.** Two stops. Load at base, unload at the destination, no return.

**Settings.** Espejo → Pablo Arturo Suárez, 2.0 kg payload, one
intermediate waypoint, 6 generations at population 5, seed 42.

**Result.** 9.7 km, 7.7 min, **73.4 Wh**, ending at 91.3 % SOC.

That the delivery uses about a tenth of the usable pack is the number
worth carrying forward — it is why everything in Part 2 is possible at
all, and it is entirely a consequence of the corridor being one of the
two that fits (Case 1.1). A one-way delivery on Espejo → Lloa is a
different document.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m examples.demo_missions --case one_way
```

### Case 2.2 — Out and back

**Question.** What does returning cost, and is a round trip simply twice
a one-way?

**Shape.** Three stops: load at base, unload at the destination, return
to base and swap the pack.

**Settings.** As Case 2.1, 2.0 kg outbound and nothing back, 300 s of
service time at each stop.

**Result.** 19.4 km, 15.7 min, **136.7 Wh**, lowest SOC 83.9 %.

**A round trip costs less than twice a one-way** — 136.7 Wh against
2 × 73.4 = 146.8 Wh, about 7 % less. The return leg flies empty, and
2 kg off a 12.5 kg aircraft is a real reduction in induced drag and in
hover thrust at both ends.

That margin is small, and it is the *calm-air* answer. In wind the sign
reverses: the round trip pays the headwind leg for longer than the
tailwind leg saves, because the slow leg lasts longer. At Quito's August
mean the penalty is a few per cent; at 8 m/s it is around 25 % for this
aircraft. See Case 3.1.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m examples.demo_missions --case out_and_back
```

### Case 2.3 — Sample collection versus supply tour

**Question.** Does the order in which mass is carried matter? A
collection round flies empty outbound and heavy home; a supply tour does
the opposite. Same facilities, same total mass moved.

**Shape.** Four stops each, over Espejo, Lloa and Píntag. Collection:
leave base empty, pick up 0.7 kg at each of two centres, return heavy.
Supply: leave base with 1.4 kg, drop 0.7 kg at each, return empty. The
two visit the same facilities in the same order — **the geometry is
identical and only the payload schedule differs.**

**Settings, and why the experiment is built this way.** Two earlier
versions of this study planned each mission separately and compared the
totals. That put the optimiser in the loop twice, and its run-to-run
spread of about 15 Wh swamped an effect worth about 4 Wh. The two
versions gave opposite answers, and neither was wrong — both were noise.

More compute would not have fixed it. The fix is to **stop re-planning**:
fix the route once, then fly both payload schedules down it. The
optimiser appears once instead of twice, its noise is common to both arms
and cancels exactly, and the result is deterministic. The route is
planned for each mission so the comparison can also be made on the
other's geometry — if the sign holds on both, the conclusion does not
depend on which route was chosen.

**Result.**

| route flown | payload order | energy | SOC min |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| collection's route | collection | **527.37 Wh** | 37.4 % |
| collection's route | supply | 533.32 Wh | 36.7 % |
| supply's route | collection | **520.14 Wh** | 38.3 % |
| supply's route | supply | 521.76 Wh | 38.1 % |

**Within a fixed route the comparison is exact** — no seeds, no spread.
Collection is cheaper by 5.95 Wh (1.12 %) on its own route and by
1.62 Wh (0.31 %) on the supply tour's.

**The 4.3 Wh between those two figures is not noise in the comparison —
it is the two routes being genuinely different, and it is the same size
as the effect being measured.** Which route the optimiser settles on
matters about as much as which way round the payload is carried. That is
worth knowing before quoting either number.

**Why, and it is not what it looks like.** The obvious explanation is
about state of charge: a collection round carries its heaviest load on
its last leg, which is also its lowest charge. That explanation predicts
collection should be *worse*. It is better, so the explanation is wrong.

What actually drives it is the terrain under each leg:

| leg | km | net climb | cost of carrying payload |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Espejo → Lloa | 11.5 | **+234 m** | **0.899 Wh/kg/km** |
| Lloa → Píntag | 30.3 | −230 m | 0.248 Wh/kg/km |
| Píntag → Espejo | 23.8 | −4 m | 0.258 Wh/kg/km |

**Carrying a kilogram costs 3.6× more on the climbing leg than on the
descending ones.** Collection wins because it flies the climb to Lloa
empty and picks up on the way back down. Potential energy accounts for
about a third of the gap — mass lifted up a hill buys energy the descent
does not give back — and the rest is that a climbing leg spends longer at
climb power.

**So the rule is not "carry heavy early" or "carry heavy late". It is:
carry the mass on the legs that descend.**

### The prediction, and it holds

If the mechanism is terrain rather than mission shape, then **the answer
should reverse over facilities with no relief between them.** The mission
table above runs the same two shapes over Pablo Arturo Suárez and
Docente Calderón — both on the basin floor — and it does reverse:

| facilities | relief | collection | supply | winner |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Lloa, Píntag (mountain) | +234 m on leg 1 | 527.4 Wh | 533.3 Wh | **collection** |
| P.A. Suárez, Calderón (basin) | flat | 267.5 Wh | 264.5 Wh | **supply** |

Same aircraft, same code, same mission shapes, opposite answers. In flat
country payload ordering is close to irrelevant and whichever way it
falls is arbitrary; in mountains it is a first-order routing decision.

That also explains why two earlier versions of this study disagreed with
each other, and why neither was wrong. They were asking a question whose
answer is a property of the terrain, and reporting it as though it were a
property of the mission.

**A caution that generalises.** This is invisible to any planner that
treats the legs of a multi-stop mission as independent — which is most of
them. The cost of a leg is not a function of the leg; it is a function of
the leg *and what it is carrying*, and the two interact through the
ground profile.

### Case 2.4 — Hub and spoke, with and without a spare battery

**Question.** Does a mission that is infeasible in the air become
feasible on the ground?

**Shape.** Base to spoke, back to base, to the next spoke, back. Two
variants, differing *only* in whether the base has a charged pack ready
when the aircraft touches down between spokes.

**Settings.** Base Espejo, spokes Pablo Arturo Suárez and Docente
Calderón, 2.0 kg per spoke, 300 s of service.

**Result.**

Spokes Lloa and Píntag — the long pair, chosen so the pack is actually
tested:

| ground equipment | energy | flight time | SOC min | verdict |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| spare pack at base | 609.1 Wh | 49.7 min | **55.7 %** | feasible |
| no spare pack | 609.1 Wh | 49.7 min | **27.8 %** | feasible |

On the basin-floor pair used in the table above (Pablo Arturo Suárez and
Docente Calderón) the same comparison gives 70.0 % against 53.9 %.

**A correction to an earlier version of this document.** It reported the
no-spare variant as running the pack flat at 6.5 % SOC. That was not a
finding, it was a bug: the mission planner tracked state of charge
against a hard-coded 600 Wh pack whatever aircraft was flying, and on
the VA23's 867 Wh of usable energy that overstated depletion by 45 %.
With the pack the aircraft actually carries, **both variants are
feasible on this mission.**

The mechanism is unchanged and so is the operational point — identical
energy, identical routing, and 28 percentage points of margin between
them. What changed is the strength of the claim: ground infrastructure
buys margin here rather than deciding whether the mission exists. Extend
the tour, or fly it on a colder morning, and it decides.

**Identical energy to the tenth of a watt-hour, identical flight time,
and one of them runs the pack past its reserve.** The aircraft flies
exactly the same path in both cases. What differs is the state of charge
it flies each leg on. **Whether this mission exists is
decided by ground equipment, not by the aircraft or the route.** That is
the case's whole point, and it is the kind of conclusion a path planner
is not usually asked to produce.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m scripts.run_studies --only battery
```

### Case 2.5 — Shuttle

**Question.** How many deliveries can one corridor absorb in a session,
and what limits the number?

**Shape.** The same one-way delivery repeated, with a pack swap at base
between each.

**Settings.** Espejo → Pablo Arturo Suárez, three round trips, 2.0 kg
each, 300 s of service.

**Result.** 6 legs, 58.4 km, 45.7 min of flight, **414.7 Wh**, lowest SOC
83.4 %, three pack swaps.

Compare that with the hub-and-spoke round in Case 2.4: **58.4 km against
53.7 km, and it ends at 83.4 % SOC instead of 53.9 %.** Nearly the same
distance and more energy, but barely a sixth of the charge consumed at
the worst point — because the shuttle returns to base between every
delivery and swaps there, so no leg ever starts on a depleted pack.

That is the throughput answer. With a swap at every base visit the pack
is not the limit and the number of flights per session is set by
turnaround time on the ground. Without one, the shuttle becomes Case 2.4
repeated and the pack is the limit again.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m examples.demo_missions --case shuttle
```

### Mission cases at a glance

All run with the VA23 at 6 generations, population 5, seed 42, one
intermediate waypoint. The effort is low deliberately — these are for
comparing shapes, not for quoting energies to three figures.

| case | legs | km | min | Wh | Wh/km | SOC min | pack swaps |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| one-way delivery | 1 | 9.7 | 7.7 | 73.4 | 7.6 | 91.3 % | 0 |
| out and back | 2 | 19.4 | 15.7 | 136.7 | 7.0 | 83.9 % | 1 |
| sample collection | 3 | 36.9 | 29.8 | 267.5 | 7.2 | 68.4 % | 1 |
| supply tour | 3 | 36.6 | 29.1 | 264.5 | 7.2 | 68.8 % | 1 |
| hub and spoke, spare pack | 4 | 53.7 | 40.3 | 390.7 | 7.3 | 70.0 % | 2 |
| hub and spoke, no spare | 4 | 53.7 | 40.3 | 390.7 | 7.3 | **53.9 %** | 0 |
| shuttle ×3 | 6 | 58.4 | 45.7 | 414.7 | 7.1 | 83.4 % | 3 |

Two things to read off that table.

**Energy per kilometre is nearly constant** — 7.0 to 7.6 Wh/km down the
whole column, a spread of 8 % across mission shapes that differ by a
factor of six in leg count. On these corridors the mission shape barely
changes the cost of covering ground. What it changes is everything else.

**State of charge is what separates the shapes.** The one-way lands at
89 % and the hub-and-spoke round at 60 %, and the hub-and-spoke only
manages that because it swaps packs twice. Take the swaps away and the same route ends at 53.9 % on these two
basin-floor spokes, and at **27.8 %** on the mountain pair the study uses
— see Case 2.4. **The mission's limit is not
energy per kilometre; it is how much charge is available at the start of
each leg**, and that is a question about ground equipment.

## Part 3 — Sensitivity cases

These do not plan a new mission. They take one and ask what it depends
on. A result that survives them is worth quoting; one that does not is
worth qualifying.

### Case 3.1 — Wind

**Question.** How much of the feasibility of these routes depends on the
air being still, and how much wind does Quito actually have?

**Shape.** A planned route re-evaluated against a steady wind field.
Wind is applied *after* planning, deliberately: the point is to measure
how much margin a route has, and a route that is only feasible in calm
air is worth knowing about even when it cannot be fixed.

**Settings.** Four named wind fields, all carrying their source:

| field | at the 10 m mast | in the corridor (90 m) | source |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `QUITO_CALMEST_MONTH` | 2.69 m/s | 3.70 m/s | station, April mean |
| `QUITO_ANNUAL_MEAN` | 3.08 m/s | 4.23 m/s | station, 1980–2012 annual |
| `QUITO_WINDIEST_MONTH` | 3.58 m/s | 4.92 m/s | station, August mean |
| `QUITO_REANALYSIS` | 1.65 m/s | 2.27 m/s | MERRA-2 annual, wind from the east |

**The two sources disagree and both are kept.** A thirty-year record at
the old Mariscal Sucre station gives 3.08 m/s from the north-north-west;
WeatherSpark's MERRA-2 reanalysis gives 1.65 m/s from the *east*. They
agree on the season and differ by a factor of 1.9 in speed and 250° in
direction. Neither is wrong — a reanalysis cell is tens of kilometres
across and cannot see a valley, and a single station sees the flow its
own valley channels. The defaults take the station magnitudes; direction
is a parameter to sweep, not a number to trust.

**Result.** Time penalty on four corridors, against the mast reading:

| corridor | calm | 2.0 m/s | 3.08 m/s | 4.0 m/s | 6.0 m/s | 8.0 m/s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Espejo → P.A. Suárez | 0 s | +43 s | +71 s | +99 s | +174 s | +278 s |
| Espejo → Calderón | 0 s | +39 s | +67 s | +93 s | +167 s | +273 s |
| Garcés → Lloa | 0 s | +27 s | +48 s | +68 s | +131 s | +237 s |
| Espejo → Píntag | 0 s | **−86 s** | **−123 s** | −152 s | −206 s | −252 s |

Two findings.

**Wind costs time, not feasibility, on these routes.** Nothing becomes
untrackable even at 8 m/s — the VA23's own rated limit — because cruise
is 26.8 m/s and the crab angle at 8 m/s of crosswind is 18°, which is
manageable. The regulatory picture does not change; the schedule does.

**One corridor gets faster.** Espejo → Píntag runs south-east, and a
north-north-westerly is a tailwind on it. That asymmetry is why round
trips are the interesting case: a one-way leg can win, a round trip
cannot, because the headwind leg lasts longer than the tailwind leg
saves.

**A methodological note that changes the numbers by half.** An airport
anemometer stands on mown grass; the corridor crosses a city. Reading a
3.08 m/s mast measurement straight up to 90 m using the *corridor's*
roughness gives 6.02 m/s. Converting properly — up from the mast on the
site's own roughness to a blending height where the flow has forgotten
the surface, then back down on the corridor's — gives 3.96 m/s. A 47 %
difference, and it propagates into every crab angle and round-trip
penalty computed from it. `WindField` carries both roughnesses for this
reason.


straight up with the corridor's roughness — it crosses the legal band at
roughly half the height the correct conversion does. Middle: the
round-trip penalty is quadratic in wind speed, so the shaded band of
Quito's monthly means costs almost nothing while the tail costs a great
deal. Right: both sources sit well inside all three airframes' rated
limits, which is why wind here costs schedule rather than feasibility.*

### Case 3.2 — Permit weight

**Question.** RDAC 101.190(a) distinguishes airspace that needs an
authorisation from airspace that is closed. An authorisation is a cost,
not a barrier. What is it worth in metres of detour?

**Shape.** One route, planned repeatedly with the permit penalty swept
from zero to a weight high enough that the optimiser flies around
instead of filing.

**Settings.** Garcés → Calderón, two intermediate waypoints, permit
weight in {0, 0.02, 0.1, 0.5, 2.0}, seed 11.

Garcés rather than Espejo, deliberately: **Espejo's pad sits inside the
Baca Ortiz hospital buffer**, so every route from it needs that permit
whatever the weight, and a sweep run from there measures nothing. The
distinction the study exists to make is between permits a route chooses
to incur and permits its endpoints impose — `unavoidable_permits` reports
the latter separately for this reason.

**Result.**

| permit weight | energy | detour | permits | standing |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 0.00 | 165.6 Wh | 1.12× | 3 | authorised |
| 0.02 | 208.2 Wh | 1.24× | 2 | authorised |
| 0.10 | 238.2 Wh | 1.29× | 1 | authorised |
| 0.50 | 196.5 Wh | 1.17× | **0** | **unsafe** — 9 waypoints below the corridor floor |
| 2.00 | 192.5 Wh | 1.17× | **0** | waiver — +24 m AGL over 3 % of route |

The switching point sits between weights of 0.10 and 0.50: below it the
optimiser crosses permit airspace and files for it, above it it routes
around. **Shedding permits costs energy monotonically up to that point**
— 166 → 208 → 238 Wh as the permit count falls 3 → 2 → 1, a 44 % energy
premium and a 29 % longer route to drop two authorisations. That is what
a permit is worth in flying, and it is the number an operator would want
when deciding whether to pursue a standing one.

**The row that matters most is the 0.50 one, and it is a warning.** With
the permit penalty set that high the optimiser found a route with zero
permits — and put nine waypoints below the 60 m corridor floor to do it,
the lowest at 58 m. It traded a safety constraint for a regulatory one.

Notice that this row is also *cheaper* than the two above it, at 196.5 Wh
against 238.2. A table sorted on energy and permits alone would rank it
best in both columns.

This is why the package separates `safe` from `flyable` and reports
`UNSAFE` rather than a permit count. **A validation that reports only
what it was asked about will certify whatever it was not asked about**,
and a penalty weight tuned to produce a desirable-looking compliance
summary can produce an undesirable route.

At weight 2.00 the optimiser instead accepts a small height waiver
(+19 m over 2 % of the route) and stays safe — which is the intended
behaviour, and the reason waivers are quantified rather than treated as
pass/fail.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m scripts.run_studies --only permits
```

### Case 3.3 — Operator credentials

**Question.** The same route flown by two operators with different
paperwork. What changes?

**Shape.** One route, evaluated with and without a specified
authorisation already in hand.

**Settings.** Espejo → Guamaní, holding versus not holding the Baca Ortiz
hospital-zone authorisation.

**Settings.** Espejo → Guamaní under three credential sets, 15
generations at population 6, seed from the study.

**Result.**

| credentials | energy | permits | standing |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| no UOC, VLOS only | 140.3 Wh | 1 | authorised — **but two blocking rules apply** |
| UOC, BVLOS | 140.3 Wh | 1 | authorised |
| UOC + hospital permits held | **114.4 Wh** | **0** | **compliant** |

Three findings.

**Credentials can block a mission that the geometry allows.** The VLOS
row has the same route and the same energy as the BVLOS one, and it
cannot be flown: RDAC 101.170(a)(1)(ii) requires a UOC to carry blood,
medicines or biological samples, and 101.310(a) forbids the open category
from BVLOS and commercial operation. **Neither of those is visible in the
route.** A planner reporting only geometry and energy would have called
this mission fine.

**An authorisation in hand is worth 26 Wh, or 18 %.** Holding the
hospital-zone permits lets the optimiser fly through airspace it would
otherwise route around, and the route gets shorter as well as legal. This
is the other side of Case 3.2: there, permits were priced as a cost to be
avoided; here, one already held costs nothing and stops being a detour
worth flying.

**And a correction.** An earlier version of this table had the third row
*more* expensive than the second — fewer constraints costing more energy,
which is the wrong way round for any causal story. It was reported as it
came out rather than smoothed, and it turned out to be one of the mission
planner bugs: the payload was being dropped from the planner's own energy
re-analysis. With that fixed the ordering is the physical one.

```bash

```

### Case 3.4 — DEM resolution

**Question.** How much of the corridor result is the terrain, and how
much is the grid it was measured on?

**Shape.** The corridor atlas re-run at 30 m, 90 m and 180 m. The coarse
grids are the shipped 30 m DEM *subsampled*, not a separate product —
decimation changes the spacing and nothing else, so any difference is
resolution alone, with no datum, void-filling policy or acquisition date
confounding it.

**Result.** Ceiling exceedance falls as the grid coarsens, on every
corridor. Espejo → Calderón goes from 82 m of exceedance at 30 m to 4 m
at 180 m: **a coarse DEM would have declared that corridor nearly
compliant.** The mechanism is that a coarse grid averages away the ridges
that bind a terrain-following corridor, and it errs in the optimistic
direction every time.

**Reproduce.**

```bash
python -m scripts.make_figures --only dem
```

**Figure.**

![What DEM resolution does to the answer](../figures/current/dem_resolution_effect.png)

*Every bar falls as the grid coarsens, on every corridor, without
exception. That one-sidedness is the point: a coarse DEM does not add
noise to a corridor verdict, it biases it optimistic, because averaging
removes ridges and never adds them.*

## What the cases add up to

Ten cases, and five conclusions that survive all of them.

### 1. The ceiling binds, not the floor

Every corridor failure in this document is an aircraft that cannot stay
*under* 122 m AGL while following ground that climbs faster than it can.
Terrain clearance — the constraint most people expect to bind — is
satisfied everywhere.

That is a consequence of how the rule is written rather than of the
geography. A ceiling measured *above ground level* turns every hill into
an obstacle for an aircraft that has to both clear it and stay near it,
and the steeper the ground the narrower the corridor becomes. In flat
country a 122 m AGL ceiling is generous. Over the Andes it is the whole
problem.

### 2. Feasibility tracks relief per kilometre, not distance

A 5.3 km leg in the atlas fails over 56 % of its length; a 23 km leg
misses by 11 m. Sizing a delivery network from a map and a range figure
gets this wrong, and gets it wrong *optimistically*, because the short
legs look easy and the short legs here are the ones that climb.

### 3. The mission's limit is charge at the start of each leg, not energy per kilometre

Across every mission shape in Part 2, energy per kilometre sits between
7.0 and 7.6 Wh/km — an 8 % spread across shapes differing by a factor of
six in leg count. What separates the shapes is the state of charge each
leg begins on, and that is settled on the ground: Case 2.4 is the same
route and the same energy to the tenth of a watt-hour, with 28
percentage points of margin between the variant that has a charged pack
waiting and the one that does not.

**A path planner is not the right tool for the decision this network
actually turns on**, and that is worth saying in a document about a path
planner. What the planner can do is price the alternatives — and Case 2.5
shows the price: a shuttle that swaps at every base visit covers more
distance than the hub-and-spoke round and finishes with 30 points more
margin.

### 4. Terrain decides more than the aircraft does

The findings most likely to be carried away from this document all turn
on relief rather than on hardware:

- Feasibility tracks relief per kilometre, not distance (Case 1.1).
- A coarse DEM biases corridor verdicts optimistic on every corridor
  without exception (Case 3.4).
- Payload ordering is a terrain question — carry the mass downhill — and
  **the answer reverses between mountain and basin facilities** with the
  same aircraft and the same code (Case 2.3).

That last one is the sharpest version of the point. The package was
built to optimise paths for an aircraft, and most of what it has to say
is about the ground underneath it.

### 5. A compliance summary can be gamed, and the model has to say so

Case 3.2 is the caution. Raise the permit penalty far enough and the
optimiser finds a route with zero permits by putting seventeen waypoints
below the corridor floor. It traded a safety constraint for a regulatory
one, and a report showing only the permit count would have made that the
best-looking row in the table.

Two protections follow, and both are in the reports rather than in the
optimiser: `safe` is reported separately from `flyable`, so no amount of
paperwork can make an unsafe route look filed; and every compliance
verdict now names the rules it did **not** test, because RDAC 101's
separation and visibility limits need a building layer and a weather feed
that this package does not have.

**Tuning a penalty weight until the compliance summary looks good is a
way to produce a route nobody should fly.**

## What these results are not

Worth stating plainly, because the tables above look more certain than
the inputs behind them.

**The aircraft are derived, not measured.** Their aspect ratios, rotor
diameters, cell type and pack overhead are engineering assumptions —
tagged as such in `ac.provenance`, and each one propagates. The pack
overhead in particular moved the VA23 from a 30 Ah pack to a 22 Ah one,
which is a 24 % change in every energy margin here.

**The wind has two sources a factor of 1.9 apart.** That is not a
climatology. It is enough to say wind costs schedule rather than
feasibility on these corridors, and not enough to plan a timetable from.

**The optimiser settings in this document are low.** Six to fifteen
generations, populations of five to eight. That is right for comparing
shapes and wrong for quoting energies to three figures. Case 2.3 is the
exception and it is deterministic by construction.

**The regulatory model covers the rules that are geometric.** Heights,
zones, distances, credentials. Not separation from people, not
visibility, not anything requiring a live feed.

### The four-panel view

![All five planning methods on one corridor](../figures/current/method_comparison.png)

*Four panels, and it takes all four. The map says where the aircraft
goes. **Altitude** says what it is actually doing — and this is the
panel that was missing: in the height-above-ground panel below it, the
blue constant-altitude route appears to climb and dive violently, when

*All three profiles now share one x-axis — distance flown by each route.
They used to disagree: the deviation panel was drawn against distance
along the direct line, which is a shorter axis, so lining the panels up
vertically compared points that were not the same point.*

**Reading the A\* row honestly.** A\* is not an unconstrained search
being compared like-for-like with the others. **Its altitude is
hard-constrained by construction** — grid nodes only exist between
terrain + 50 m and terrain + 120 m — so it cannot represent a ceiling
violation, and it wins that column by having no way to lose it. Airspace
enters as a soft 50 Wh penalty. Aircraft dynamics barely enter at all:
no turn-radius limit, no climb-angle limit between nodes, a fixed

phase-specific ones. It also returns bare waypoints rather than flight
segments, so its energy comes from its own edge-cost model and is not
comparable with the others — which is why the energy column shows "—"
for it.

### The route in three dimensions

![The route over the terrain in three dimensions](../figures/cases/corridor/garces-pintag/path_3d.png)

*The plan and profile views each drop an axis and leave the reader to
put them back. This does not — and it is the worst view for reading a

### What the optimiser did, and why

![The routes the search tried, and the objective behind them](../figures/cases/corridor/garces-pintag/optimizer_history.png)

*Left: every route the search kept, faded early and solid late. Right —
and this is the half that was missing — **the objective plotted against
the constraint penalties that make it up.**

A convergence curve on its own is not evidence. It says the objective
fell; it does not say whether the fall came from finding a cheaper route
or from giving up on a constraint. In the run shown, energy falls from
258 Wh to 191 Wh while a permit penalty rises at generation 8, holds,
and drops to zero at 13 — the search briefly accepted an authorisation
to get a cheaper route, then found one that shed it.

**A search whose objective drops while a penalty rises is not
converging; it is trading legality for energy.** That is precisely the
failure the permit-sensitivity study catches at high penalty weight
(Case 3.2), and this figure is where it would be visible on any single
run.*

### The pack, through the flight

![State of charge, voltage, current and energy over one flight](../figures/cases/out_and_back/garces-lloa/battery.png)

*Four panels on one time axis with the flight phases shaded behind all
of them — a two-leg out-and-back, so the mid-mission landing and the
climb-out for the return are both visible as rotor-borne bands.*

*The panels are not restatements of each other. **Terminal voltage** is
not state of charge: it sags under load and recovers, and it is what a
low-voltage cutout actually watches. **Current** shows the hover phases
immediately — the peak is a rotor-borne phase every single time.
**Cumulative energy** separates what reached the drivetrain from what
became heat in the pack.*

*Rotor-borne phases are always shaded however short they are. The
fixed-wing slivers are filtered out: a terrain-following leg is forty
alternating climbs and descents, and shading each one turns the
background into a wash that hides exactly the two blocks the panel
exists to point at.*

### Where the energy went, against where the time went

![Energy share against time share, by flight phase](../figures/cases/out_and_back/garces-lloa/energy_breakdown.png)

*The single most useful number on this figure is the ratio above each
pair. **VTOL ascent is ×3.7 and VTOL descent ×3.2** — they take that
many times more of the battery than of the clock. Fixed-wing descent is
×0.3, nearly free.*

*This is the arithmetic behind every multi-stop result in Part 2. An
extra stop is not an extra few kilometres; it is another descent, hover
and climb-out, and those are the phases that cost three times their
share of the flight.*

## Part 5 — Energy against time

### Case 2.6 — The same delivery, optimised three ways

**Question.** What does hurrying cost?

**Shape.** One delivery, Espejo → Píntag, 2.0 kg, planned three times:
for energy under a routine 60-minute cap, for time under an emergency
15-minute cap, and balanced under an urgent 30-minute cap.

**Result.**

| objective | urgency | cap | energy | flight time | SOC at end |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| energy | routine | 60 min | **169.6 Wh** | 16.2 min | 80.0 % |
| time | emergency | 15 min | 224.0 Wh | **15.0 min** | 73.4 % |
| balanced | urgent | 30 min | 151.6 Wh | 17.5 min | 82.2 % |

**Hurrying costs 32 % more energy to save 7 % of the time.** The
time-optimised route flies faster and straighter, paying for both in
drag, and it arrives with 6.6 percentage points less charge. On a
corridor where the pack has margin that is a good trade for a blood
delivery and a bad one for a scheduled resupply — which is the whole
reason the two objectives are separate.

**A caution on the third row.** Balanced came out *cheaper* than the
energy objective, which cannot be right: a 50/50 weighted objective
cannot beat a pure energy objective at minimising energy. At ten
generations and a population of six the search has not converged and the
spread between runs is larger than the difference. It is reported as it
came out rather than smoothed, and it should not be quoted.

### Rethinking wind

![What wind does to these routes](../figures/current/wind_sensitivity.png)

*The version this replaces drew a boundary-layer profile, a round-trip
penalty curve and a bar chart of two data sources. All three were
correct and none said anything about a corridor — a reader could not
tell from any of them which route to worry about, or on which day.*

*These three are built on the fact that makes Quito's wind interesting:
**nobody knows which way it blows.** The station record says
north-north-west, the reanalysis says east, 250° apart. So rather than
pick one, sweep the direction.*

**Left — a wind rose of cost.** Time penalty against wind direction, at
August's mean speed, with the zero circle drawn: inside it the wind is a
net help. A corridor that plots as a circle does not care which way the
wind blows. A corridor that plots as a lobe is a scheduling problem, and
**which lobe it is facing depends on which data source is right.**
Espejo→Guamaní is worst from 210°, Espejo→Calderón from 30° — so the two
candidate directions do not merely disagree, they disagree *differently
for different corridors*.

**Middle — cost against speed, each corridor at its own worst
direction.** The band marks what Quito actually has. Inside it the
penalties are under a minute; the curves only turn steep well past the
airframe's Beaufort-5 limit, where the aircraft should not be flying
anyway. **Wind here costs schedule, not feasibility** — and that
conclusion is robust across the whole direction sweep, which is why it
survives the two sources disagreeing.

**Right — the round trip loses both ways.** Every corridor has one leg
that *gains* from the wind: Espejo→Guamaní is 1.05 min faster outbound.
And every net is still positive, because the slow leg lasts longer than
the fast leg saves. That asymmetry is why a one-way delivery and a round
trip are different planning problems, and it is invisible in any figure
that averages over a mission.
